=== d270 138:6.2

E eles seguiram esse plano de descanso de um dia por semana durante o resto da vida material do seu Mestre. Como regra geral, às quartas-feiras eles nunca se dedicavam às suas atividades regulares. T: The Master Who Gave Rest
[0] After all twelve apostles had been chosen, Jesus held an intensive week of training for them. It was at this time that Jesus established the mid-week holiday for rest and recreation, and they kept to this plan throughout the remainder of his earth life. As a general rule, they never prosecuted their regular activities on Wednesday. Even though they stood before the immense mission of establishing the kingdom of heaven, Jesus made rest a part of the ministry. This scene resonates deeply with those of us who live overly busy lives today. Jesus knew that the greater the mission, the greater the need for rest.
[1] The words Jesus spoke to the apostles are truly warm: “⟦Q 138:6.2⟧My children, go for a day of play.⟦/Q⟧” He urged the apostles to rest from the arduous labors of the kingdom and to enjoy the refreshment that comes from reverting to their former vocations or from discovering new sorts of recreational activity. There is no strictness masquerading as piety here. Jesus did not regard play and rest as opposed to the spiritual life. On the contrary, he knew that rest and enjoyment renew people and give them strength to work again.
[2] What is interesting is that, while Jesus himself did not actually require this day of rest, he conformed to this plan because he knew it was best for his human associates. He deeply understood the apostles’ human limitations. People who work without rest grow weary, and weary people have clouded judgment and dried-up hearts. Jesus did not demand superhuman devotion from his disciples, but saw to it that they could serve long and in good health as human beings. This is the wisdom of a true leader. He treated his disciples not as tools for work but as beloved children.
[3] This teaching connects with The Urantia Book’s consistent message about a balanced life. Jesus was not an ascetic (see Paper 136); he wanted body, mind, and spirit to be healthy together. He not only worked, taught, and prayed with the apostles but also rested and laughed with them. Spiritual growth comes about healthily not amid tension and overwork but in a life where work and rest, service and renewal, are in harmony. There is no need to feel guilty about resting. Jesus’ own life was an example in which work, prayer, and rest were beautifully woven together.
[4] We often take being busy as proof of faithfulness and resting as laziness. Even in the life of faith, some people are worn out by the pressure to serve more and gather more. But Jesus urged rest upon his own. Rest is not only a time to stop working but a time to be renewed and to enjoy the joy of life the Father has given us. Those who rest well can serve longer and more joyfully. To exhaust oneself by refusing rest may be not humility but an impatience that fails to trust the Father’s care.
[5] This week, set aside a Wednesday of your own. On that day, step away from a sense of duty, do something you love, walk in nature, and spend time laughing with family or friends. And thank the Father who has allowed you that rest. Jesus’ warm voice saying, “⟦Q 138:6.2⟧Go for a day of play,⟦/Q⟧” reaches us today as well. Gladly accepting that invitation is also a form of faith, and a well-rested heart gains strength to take up again, with joy, the work of the kingdom.
PR: Set aside time this week to rest and enjoy yourself free from duty, and savor that rest with a grateful heart.

T: Faith, the Open Door
[0] During the period when the twelve apostles first worked together, Jesus taught them plainly about the way to enter the kingdom of heaven. The Jews of that time were taught that they had to repent and win God’s favor through good works and observance of the law. John the Baptist, too, had cried out for repentance in order to flee from the wrath to come. But in this passage Jesus presents an entirely different way: faith is the only requisite to entering the Father’s kingdom, and that faith is the open door for entering into the present, perfect, and eternal love of God.
[1] This difference is very important. The repentance of good works is an attempt by a person to earn worthiness before God by his own efforts. Such faith is always anxious, because it endlessly asks, “Am I good enough? Have I repented enough?” But the change of mind by faith that Jesus taught, the new birth, begins with believing in and accepting the love of God. We do not do good in order to win love; we come to do good because we are already loved.
[2] This shift from fear to love is the heart of Jesus’ gospel. John’s message leaned on fear of wrath, but Jesus’ message is founded on assurance of the Father’s love. Fear makes people shrink; love sets them free. To say that God’s love is present means that this love is not deferred as a future reward but can be enjoyed now. To say that it is perfect and eternal means that our mistakes do not diminish it. The Father’s love is not a prize we must earn but a gift already given.
[3] This passage also tells us that Jesus spoke as one having authority. He did not speak like a prophet who comes to declare the word of God, but as a Son who knew the Father directly. And he sought to divert the minds of those who longed for miracles to the finding of a real and personal experience in the indwelling of God’s spirit of love and saving grace. The assurance of faith comes not from marvelous outward signs but from personal communion with the spirit within us. A heart that seeks signs always wants more signs, but a heart in communion with the spirit abides in quiet assurance.
[4] We too sometimes live under pressure to prove something in order to win God’s love. There is an anxiety that we must pray more, serve more, and become more perfect before God will accept us. But Jesus says the door is already open. What opens that door is not our merit but the Father’s love, and the way through it is simple faith. When we accept this, our life of faith is changed from a burden of duty into the joy of gratitude. There is no need to hesitate at the door, weighing our qualifications.
[5] Today, lay down the urge to prove yourself before God. Accept with simple faith the fact that the Father’s love is here and now, perfect and eternal. And do today’s good works out of the gratitude that flows from that love. Good works done from fear and good works done from love may look alike, but to the soul that does them, the difference is as great as that between heaven and earth.
PR: Today, do one good deed not to win God’s approval but out of gratitude for the love you have already received.

T: The Flame Rekindled
[0] Paper 139 frankly portrays the character, strengths, and weaknesses of each of the twelve apostles. Among them, Simon Peter was impulsive and optimistic, easily swept along by strong emotions. He confessed Jesus as the Messiah before anyone else, yet on the night Jesus was arrested he denied his Master three times. This passage tells us what happened to Peter after that bitter failure. It is one of the most hopeful scenes in The Urantia Book, showing that failure need not be the end but can become a new beginning.

With Andrew’s sympathetic and understanding guidance, Peter again led the way back to the fish nets. What a fallen person needs is not blame but understanding and companionship. Andrew did not drag up his brother’s failure but stayed beside him so that he could return to the place of daily life and catch his breath. There are fallen people around us too. To become a brother like Andrew to them is itself the work of the kingdom.

A soul crushed by guilt can hardly find strength, however hard it tries. But the assurance of being forgiven sets the soul free, and new strength wells up from that liberated soul. The Father’s forgiveness is already prepared before we ask for it, and only when we accept it by faith does it become a living experience within us (see Paper 174).

Peter’s bold proclamation of the gospel at Pentecost was the fruit of precisely this restoration. What is remarkable is that the power that made him so useful was not his outstanding eloquence but the humility he had gained by passing through failure and the gratitude of one who had been forgiven. Those who have fallen deeply are often the ones who best understand the hearts of the fallen.
[4] Peter’s story gives us new eyes with which to view failure. When we fail, we are quick to condemn ourselves and to think we can never be used again. But the Father does not regard our failures as a final verdict. On the contrary, through those failures he shapes us into people who are humbler, deeper, and more useful. The question is not whether we have fallen, but whether, in the place where we fell, we accept forgiveness and rise again. Peter’s life bears witness that even one who has failed can become a shining vessel in the Father’s hands.
[5] If today you are imprisoning yourself because of past failures, remember Peter. Do not doubt the Father’s forgiveness; accept it. And if there is someone like Andrew beside you who understands you, be thankful; and if you can be such a person for someone else, gladly take up that role. When the assurance of forgiveness settles in the soul, the fires of the kingdom will burn brightly within us again. And that flame will light up even the darkness of others.
PR: Entrust to the Father one past failure that holds you back, and decide how you will rise again today as one who is forgiven.

T: Thomas Who Overcame Doubt
[0] Later ages came to know him as “doubting Thomas,” but The Urantia Book sees him in an entirely different light. This passage calls Thomas the great example of a human being who has doubts, faces them, and wins. He was no carping critic but a logical thinker with a great mind. Thomas’s doubt was not the enemy of faith but an honest question testing whether faith was genuine. And having followed that question honestly to the very end, he believed in Jesus of Nazareth.

The faith of a doubting person actually carries more weight than the faith of one who believes easily. Scientists may not fully understand all about Jesus and his work on earth, but a man whose mind was that of a true scientist lived and worked with the Master and believed in him. Science and faith are not enemies. A mind that respects facts and honestly searches for truth will in the end arrive before the One who is living truth (see Paper 195). But Thomas’s story tells us that there is no reason for honest doubt to be shut out of the path of faith. What matters is not whether we have doubts, but whether we face them and go on seeking the truth. Do not hide or suppress your doubts; bring them to the Spirit of Truth and wrestle with them honestly. The Father never turns away from a child who asks honestly.

[0] Paper 139, on the twelve apostles, ends with the story of Judas Iscariot. Judas was a capable businessman and served as treasurer of the apostolic corps, yet in the end he betrayed his Master. Many people ask whether Jesus did not know what kind of man Judas was. This passage answers clearly: from the beginning the Master fully understood the weakness of this apostle and well knew the dangers of admitting him to fellowship. Even so, Jesus received him. To Jesus, Judas was a faith adventure.

The other is vigilance about our own choices. We are tempted to keep our distance in advance from people who seem likely to disappoint us, people whose weaknesses are plain to see. But Jesus gave a chance even while knowing the risk. If there is someone you have been quick to give up on, venture once more on an adventure of faith with that person. And if disappointment has piled up in your heart, entrust it to the Father before it hardens into resentment. This is the way to carry on in our own lives the adventure of faith that Jesus undertook.

T: Save, Do Not Judge
[0] In January of A.D. 27, on the highland near Capernaum, Jesus ordained the twelve apostles as ambassadors of the kingdom. This passage is part of the sermon he gave them right after the ordination. Jesus tells the apostles plainly: “⟦Q 140:3.17⟧You are commissioned to save men, not to judge them.⟦/Q⟧” These words set the fundamental attitude that workers of the kingdom must hold. One who proclaims the gospel is not a judge but a messenger bearing the news of salvation.
[1] Jesus explains the reason in very human terms: at the end of your earth life you will all expect mercy. We are all beings who will one day ask for mercy. How contradictory it would be, then, if we ourselves applied a strict yardstick of judgment to others. So Jesus requires that during our mortal life we show mercy to all our brethren in the flesh. Those who hope for mercy must first show mercy. Those who remember the mercy they have received do not find it hard to show mercy to others.
[2] Jesus then gives the parable of the beam and the mote. It is a mistake to try to pluck a mote out of your brother’s eye when there is a beam in your own eye. We are quick to spot the small faults of others but slow to see our own large ones. The stronger the urge to judge others, the more likely it is a sign that we lack self-examination. Jesus does not tell us not to help our brother. He says that having first cast the beam out of our own eye, we can the better see to cast the mote out of our brother’s eye. Self-examination is the precondition for helping others.
[3] This teaching touches on the teaching about the Father’s love that follows in the same Paper. Jesus wanted his disciples to treat people not merely with brotherly love but with the love of a father. The Father’s love does not condone evil, but it always looks for the best in people. The judging eye sees a person’s failures; the saving eye sees the possibilities within that person. Just as the Father in heaven makes his sun shine on the good and the evil alike, we are called to shine the light of mercy on all.
[4] In our lives today, too, moments when we want to judge come again and again. Seeing a coworker’s mistakes, a family member’s shortcomings, or the wrongdoings of people in the news, we readily pass sentence. But such sentences rarely lift anyone up. What changes people is not condemnation but understanding and mercy. Jesus won the souls of tax collectors and sinners by eating and drinking with them. He almost never changed anyone with words of condemnation. Mercy is not the abandonment of justice but the fulfillment of justice in love.
[5] Today, when you feel the urge to judge someone, stop for a moment. First ask yourself quietly what the beam in your own eye might be. Then look at that person with eyes of salvation and restoration, and think about what words or deeds would help him. The heart of one who shows mercy grows wider, and into that widened heart the Father’s mercy flows more abundantly. In this way we live our day not as judges but as messengers of salvation.
PR: When you feel like judging someone, first examine the beam in your own eye, and then offer that person one word that lifts them up.

The first subject was doing the Father’s will. Jesus taught that trusting in the overcare of the heavenly Father is not a blind and passive fatalism. This distinction is very important for keeping the life of faith healthy.
[1] Jesus taught people to consider the birds of the air and the lilies of the field, and not to be anxious about tomorrow. But if these words are misunderstood, they can lead to an attitude of doing nothing and simply waiting for God to do everything. So Jesus quoted with approval an old Hebrew saying: “⟦Q 140:8.2⟧He who will not work shall not eat.⟦/Q⟧” To trust the Father does not mean to fold our hands. [2] Jesus pointed to his own experience as sufficient commentary on his teachings. Indeed, when we look at his life this balance shows up clearly. After losing his father Joseph in his boyhood, he took responsibility for the family as the eldest son and worked faithfully as a carpenter and a boatbuilder (see Papers 127 and 129). Yet he was never gripped by anxiety, and he always trusted the Father’s care. Diligent labor and deep trust were woven together as one in his life.

His instruction embraces the ideal principles of living near God in all ages and on all worlds. Economic systems and ways of life change from age to age, but the principle of a life that trusts the Father and works faithfully does not change. Whether we live as farmers, as office workers, or as those who care for a home, each of us can live out this principle where we are. Whatever our circumstances, whoever trusts the Father and works faithfully lives near God right where he is.

On the other is a life that evades responsibility under the pretext of faith. Jesus shows us a way beyond both. It is to do our own part with all our strength and to entrust to the Father what lies beyond our strength. This is the peaceful diligence of one who does the Father’s will. [0] In the days of the new school of the prophets at Bethsaida, Thomas asked Jesus the difference between evil, sin, and iniquity. In answering, Jesus corrected a misunderstanding widespread at the time: the idea that man is by nature estranged from God, an alien or stepchild, who can become part of God’s family only by going through a legal process of adoption. Jesus said that such a concept arose from a misunderstanding of the Father and from ignorance of the origin, nature, and destiny of man (148:4.8).
[1] As evidence to the contrary, Jesus reminds Thomas of a number of passages in the Hebrew scriptures. Words such as “⟦Q 148:4.10⟧You are the children of the Lord your God⟦/Q⟧” and “⟦Q 148:4.10⟧They who have the spirit of God are indeed the sons of God⟦/Q⟧” had been written long before. People had simply forgotten that truth beneath the weight of law and ritual. Jesus did not invent a new doctrine; he drew out the most precious truth buried in the scriptures and set it once more in the light.
[2] The last sentence of the passage sums up this truth in a beautiful figure: “⟦Q 148:4.10⟧While there is a material part of the human father in the natural child, there is a spiritual part of the heavenly Father in every faith son of the kingdom.⟦/Q⟧” The Urantia Book calls this spiritual part the Thought Adjuster. That we are children of God is not merely a legal title but a real spiritual lineage. Between God and us, therefore, there is from the very beginning a spiritual bond that cannot be broken.

He acknowledged that man is by nature evil, that is, imperfect and immature, and he said that rebirth of the spirit is essential to entering the kingdom of heaven. Evil is a deviation arising from ignorance or immaturity regarding God’s will; sin is the deliberate choice to go against it knowingly; and iniquity is the persistent repetition of that choice (148:4.2). We are born with imperfection, but whether we become sinners depends on our choices. Imperfection is not a wall that separates us from the Father, but a starting point to be overcome together with the Father’s spirit.

There is no need to begin our faith in guilt and fear. Rather, the way of faith is to trust the Father’s spirit within us and to respond to its guidance a little more each day. The way to God is not a journey in search of a distant palace, but the act of listening to the One who has already come to dwell within us.
[5] And this truth applies equally to others. The Father’s spirit dwells within the people I meet as well. Even if they do not yet know it, they are sons and daughters of God in the making. Therefore we can treat no one as a person with whom God has nothing to do. May this be a day of remembering the Father’s spirit within, both before the mirror and before the faces of our neighbors.

T: He Does Not Send Affliction
[0] Nathaniel had long wrestled with the purpose of affliction. It was hard for him to understand why good people suffer and why God permits it. Before anything else, Jesus makes one thing clear to him: “⟦Q 148:5.3⟧the Father does not purposely afflict his children.⟦/Q⟧” This simple declaration shakes to the roots many misconceptions about suffering, above all the old notion that behind every misfortune lies God’s punishment. The problem of suffering is one of the hardest questions believers of every age have had to face.
[1] Jesus carefully distinguishes the causes of affliction. “⟦Q 148:5.3⟧The imperfections and handicaps of evil are inherent⟦/Q⟧” in an evolving world; “⟦Q 148:5.3⟧the penalties of sin are inevitable; the destroying consequences of iniquity are inexorable.⟦/Q⟧” And “⟦Q 148:5.3⟧man brings down upon himself unnecessary affliction as a result of his persistent refusal to walk in the better ways of the divine will.⟦/Q⟧” Yet in none of these cases does God “⟦Q 148:5.3⟧send affliction as an arbitrary punishment for wrongdoing.⟦/Q⟧” Affliction is for the most part the natural result of universe laws interlocking with human choices.
[2] In the section that follows, Jesus interprets the book of Job and stresses the same lesson (148:6). Job’s friends insisted that suffering was proof of sin, but that was a misunderstanding of God. Jesus also warned against the tendency to lay at God’s door everything people do not understand (148:5.4). Making God the direct cause of every event may seem to honor him, but in truth it is a road to doubting his love.
[3] What, then, should we do in the face of suffering? Jesus’ answer is remarkably practical. We should neither blame God nor complain of life, but “⟦Q 148:5.3⟧work persistently and consistently toward the betterment of [our] estate on earth.⟦/Q⟧” “⟦Q 148:5.3⟧Intelligent application would enable man to overcome much of his earthly misery.⟦/Q⟧” Disease can be reduced by medicine, poverty by wise cooperation, conflict by understanding and dialogue. It is not resigning ourselves to suffering as God’s will, but striving to overcome it, that is the Father’s will.
[4] This teaching also changes our attitude toward those who suffer. To probe for the cause when someone meets with hardship and to search out their fault is the mistake Job’s friends made. Our task is not to judge but to stand beside them, and to look together for practical ways to lighten their load. The Urantia Book says that difficulties can become part of the experience that grows the soul (154:2.5), but this does not mean glorifying suffering; it means drawing meaning even out of it.
[5] If there is something painful in your life today, first lay down the thought that God is punishing you. The Father is beside you even in that pain, and he longs to give you strength to overcome it wisely. When we choose trust instead of resentment and effort instead of resignation, affliction can become, instead of something that breaks us, a tool that shapes us into deeper and more mature souls. He is not the one who inflicts pain, but the one who walks with us through it and holds us up.
