T: Justice, Mercy, and Humility
0 Paper 97 tells how the concept of God evolved through the Hebrew prophets. Samuel proclaimed the unchanging God, Elijah the oneness of Yahweh, Amos the God of justice, and Hosea the God of love. Today’s passage shows the heart of the message Micah delivered in that unfolding story. He asks, “⟦Q 97:5.6⟧Shall I come before God with burnt offerings? Will the Lord be pleased with a thousand rams or with ten thousand rivers of oil?⟦/Q⟧” And he answers that what the Lord requires of man is “⟦Q 97:5.6⟧to do justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly with your God.⟦/Q⟧”
1 This question and answer overturn in a single stroke a history of sacrifice thousands of years long. As we saw in Paper 89, mankind long offered sacrifices to win the favor of the gods, even sacrificing their own children. Micah’s question includes the words “⟦Q 97:5.6⟧Shall I give my first-born for my transgression?⟦/Q⟧” because such a custom actually existed. But Micah declares that what God desires is not the blood of beasts, nor costly oil, nor the lives of children. What God desires is a changed life.
2 To do justly is to deal fairly with one’s neighbor: not to exploit the weak, to defend the rights of the poor, to trade without deceit. Micah sternly rebuked rulers who judged for bribes and priests who taught for hire (see Paper 97). To love mercy is to show kindness to others not as a duty but as a joy. If justice is fairness, mercy is love that goes beyond fairness. And to walk humbly with God is a life of daily companionship with him, seeking his will. These three become a mirror in which we can measure the genuineness of our own faith today.
3 These three are not separate from one another. The one who walks humbly with God naturally comes to do justly and love mercy. On the other hand, to demand justice without a relationship with God easily slides into self-righteousness, and to stress mercy alone easily drifts into sentimentality. The three that Micah set forth are the balanced shape of true religion. When Jesus later taught us to love God and to love our neighbor as ourselves, he carried forward this same spirit.
4 The passage says that it was a great age, that these were indeed stirring times when mortal man heard, and some even believed, such emancipating messages more than two and a half millenniums ago. Yet it laments that but for the stubborn resistance of the priests, these teachers would have overthrown the whole bloody ceremonial of Hebrew worship. Institutions and vested interests always stand in the way of the advance of truth. Even today, form and ritual sometimes try to take the place of a changed heart. We easily assume we have fulfilled our religious duty by attending worship and giving an offering. But Micah’s question is still being put to us.
5 Today, hold Micah’s three requirements up to your own life. Ask yourself whether you treated someone fairly today, whether you showed kindness gladly, and whether you walked humbly, seeking God’s will. Then pick one area where you fell short and resolve to do a little better tomorrow. What pleases God is not a grand offering but small, sincere changes like these in the way we live.
PR: Choose one of justice, mercy, or a humble walk with God today, and decide on one concrete act that puts it into practice.

T: Wisdom, Faith, and Experience United
0 Paper 98 recounts the long journey of the Melchizedek teachings as they flowed into the Western world, passing through Greek philosophy, the Roman state religion, and the various mystery cults, until at last they reached Christianity. Along the way the Greeks sought security of mind through rigorous thinking, but the philosophers made light of worship, and the masses flocked to mystery cults that stirred the emotions. Today’s passage looks back on this history and draws out a principle: religions have long endured without philosophical support, but few philosophies, as such, have long persisted without some identification with religion.
1 This is not a disparagement of philosophy. The passage compares the relation of philosophy to religion with that of conception to action. Conception explains action and gives it direction, but it is action that actually lives and moves. In the same way, religion is the living experience of knowing God, and philosophy is the work of thought that seeks to understand and order that experience. Philosophy without experience, however refined, cannot hold a human life together; in the end it scatters into barren speculation. This is why the brilliant philosophy of Greece failed to win the hearts of ordinary people (see Paper 98, section 2).
2 Nor does this mean that religion may remain thoughtless. The passage clearly portrays the ideal human estate: that in which philosophy, religion, and science are welded into a meaningful unity by the conjoined action of wisdom, faith, and experience. Science deals with facts, philosophy weaves meanings, and religion experiences values. When these three do not push one another away but live in harmony within one personality, a person becomes whole, with head and heart no longer divided. The integration of personality that Papers 103 and 112 repeatedly stress points in exactly this direction.
3 In daily life this teaching returns to us as very concrete questions. Am I using faith as an excuse to stop thinking? Or am I so busy piling up and weighing knowledge that I keep postponing my living communion with God? Faith without wisdom easily leans toward superstition and dogmatism; knowledge without faith easily falls into cynicism and emptiness. And without experience, both faith and knowledge become mere words.
4 This principle holds just as true today. The modern world has made astonishing scientific progress, yet in the midst of all that knowledge many people lose the meaning of life and wander. On the other side, some religious people treat science and philosophy as enemies and build walls against them, shutting their faith inside a narrow fence. The Urantia Book urges us to move beyond both of these paths. Only when the honesty of science that respects facts, the sincerity of philosophy that seeks meaning, and the vitality of religion that experiences God come together does a person begin to see the universe rightly. This unity is not won merely by reading many books; it comes little by little as wisdom, faith, and experience grow together day by day.
5 So today, let us practice examining honestly in thought what we believe, looking again at what we know in the light of faith, and carrying both into some small action. Living experience of God deepens our thinking, and deeper thinking in turn makes our experience clearer. The lesson the Western world learned with such difficulty over thousands of years we can learn anew each day in the small choices of a single day.
PR: Think honestly today about one thing you believe, and carry that belief into one small action.

Habits themselves are not bad. As Paper 100 says, good religious habits help spiritual growth. But living experience and fresh meaning must flow within those habits.
4 Paper 99 also explains why such religion is especially needed today. When society changes rapidly and economic insecurity and clashing ideologies shake people, what they need most is an unshakable inner center. Just after our passage the paper says that whatever upheavals may attend the social and economic growth of civilization, religion will survive if it is genuine and worthy. It adds that in the end conduct is determined not so much by what a person knows as by what he believes (see Paper 99, section 4). Only a living religion that faces the realities of everyday life can hold people steady in such times.

We can be grateful even while washing the dishes, keep a brotherly spirit even in a difficult conversation, and find the value of faithfulness even in tedious work. As ordinary moments are transformed in this way into the experience of knowing God, our religion will be an anchor that holds no matter how the world is shaken. The ordinary day is the most honest test of whether our religion is true, and at the same time the place where we can meet God most closely.
PR: Choose the most ordinary and tedious task of your day, and do it with care, as something done together with God.

No entanto, a mente que realmente discerne Deus, que escuta o Ajustador residente, é a mente pura. “Sem consagrar-se, nenhum homem pode ver o Senhor.” Toda a comunhão interior e espiritual é denominada discernimento espiritual. Tais experiências religiosas resultam da impressão feita na mente do homem por meio das atuações combinadas do Ajustador e do Espírito da Verdade, funcionando entre e sobre as idéias, os ideais, sobre a visão interior e os esforços espirituais dos filhos de Deus em evolução.
T: The Spirit Meets Us in Thought
0 Paper 101 deals with the real nature of religion. It says that true religion is not a philosophical system of belief that can be reasoned out or replaced by something else, but a living experience produced as the Thought Adjuster, the spirit of the Universal Father, works within the human mind. Today’s passage tells us where in us that experience takes place: the divine spirit makes contact with mortal man, not by feelings or emotions, but in the realm of the highest and most spiritualized thinking.
1 This overturns the common religious assumptions of many people. We often think we have met God only when there are fervent emotions, tears, and overwhelming feelings. But the passage says clearly: “⟦Q 101:1.3⟧It is your thoughts, not your feelings, that lead you Godward.⟦/Q⟧” Emotions rise and fall like the weather. On days when we feel good God seems near, and on gloomy days he seems far away. But the spirit of God does not come and go with our moods.
2 Yet this thinking does not mean cold calculation or the piling up of knowledge. The passage says that the divine nature may be perceived only with the eyes of the mind, and adds that the mind that really discerns God, that hears the indwelling Adjuster, is the pure mind. It also quotes the words “⟦Q 101:1.3⟧Without holiness no man may see the Lord.⟦/Q⟧” The thinking that reaches God is honest, clean, and directed toward what is high. The passage calls all such inner and spiritual communion spiritual insight.
3 The last sentence of the passage explains how religious experience arises. Such religious experiences result from the impress made upon the mind of man by the combined operations of the Adjuster and the Spirit of Truth as they function amid and upon the ideas, ideals, insights, and spirit strivings of the evolving sons of God. The paragraph just before says that the Adjuster has no special mechanism through which to gain self-expression (see Paper 101, section 1). The Adjuster works through our ordinary thoughts and ideals. So the quality of the thoughts we entertain becomes the quality of the channel through which God can work within us.
4 This teaching reminds us how important it is to cultivate our thinking. If we fill our minds all day with resentment, worry, and thoughts that belittle others, the room in which the Adjuster can work grows narrow. But when we think on truth, beauty, and goodness, cherish higher ideals, and strive to do what is right, the Adjuster’s leading grows ever clearer. We need not be discouraged even on days when our feelings run dry. On such days, too, we can choose lofty thoughts, and in that choosing God quietly works.
5 Today, let us measure our faith not by the temperature of our emotions but by the direction of our thoughts. Quietly ask yourself: What am I thinking right now? Does this thought lead me toward God, or away from him? Then consciously take hold of one noble thought: a thought of understanding someone, of living honestly, of doing the Father’s will. In that place of thought we meet the indwelling spirit. Emotion may follow after as a gift, but it is not the guide. Those who keep their thoughts lifted high are not shaken even in seasons when feelings run dry; quietly but steadily they move toward the Father.
PR: Regardless of your mood, choose one noble thought for today and bring it to mind again and again.
