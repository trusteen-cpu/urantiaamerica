ch31:title "The Origin, Development, and Future of Christianity"
ch31:0 "The Urantia Book portrays religion as the meeting place of two paths: the evolutionary path by which man seeks God and the revelatory path by which God seeks man. In the Western world that meeting bore its greatest fruit in Christianity. This chapter asks how The Urantia Book understands Christianity. Where did Christianity come from, what road has it traveled, and where must it go? The Urantia Book does not answer these questions with condemnation. It honors Christianity's contributions highly, yet it states honestly how Christianity came to differ from the religion of Jesus himself."
ch31:1 "The Urantia Book's most concise definition of Christianity appears in a passage that classifies the world religions of the twentieth century."
ch31:3 "This definition holds the key to the whole chapter. Christianity is a religion “about the life and teachings of Christ.” The religion Jesus himself lived and the religion built up about Jesus are bound together, yet they are not the same. Hold fast to this distinction, and the origin, development, and future of Christianity can be read as a single line of logic. Its origin was the passage from the religion of Jesus to a religion about Jesus; its development was the broadening of that religion through compromise with the cultures of the world; and its future is the reawakening of the religion of Jesus that lies sleeping within it. This chapter confirms these three links, one after another, from the text."
ch31:4 "1. Origin: From the Gospel of the Kingdom to the Gospel of Christ"
ch31:5 "What Jesus proclaimed was the gospel of the kingdom: the fatherhood of God and the brotherhood of man. But after the cross, the resurrection, and Pentecost, the center of the disciples' preaching shifted. The Urantia Book records this shift as the opening scene in the birth of Christianity."
ch31:7 "This change did not spring from ill will. The disciples were aflame with their experience of the risen Lord, and they regarded the telling of that experience as the very preaching of the gospel. While pointing out the mistake this first community made, The Urantia Book also speaks of its greatness."
ch31:9 "Their greatness and their mistake grew from a single root. The good will of the disciples arose not from recognizing the brotherhood of man but from a love born of the concept of Jesus' bestowal (194:4.6). They formed a fellowship of believers in Jesus, and that fellowship brought forth a marvelous brotherly love. But at the center of that love stood “the Father of the Lord Jesus” rather than “the loving Father of all men.” Paper 196 sums up this beginning in the names of two apostles."
ch31:11 "Note the word “unintentionally.” Peter did not set out to found a new religion. He simply told what he had seen, and as that telling accumulated, it became a religion. Paper 195 summarizes: “Peter was the real founder of the Christian church; Paul carried the Christian message to the gentiles, and the Greek believers carried it to the whole Roman Empire.” (195:0.1) When the Jewish people, bound by law and tradition, could not accept the new gospel, the current of religious evolution shifted westward."

ch31:14 "2. Expansion: Hellenization and Compromise"
ch31:15 "How did a message that began in Galilee capture the finest minds of the Roman Empire? The Urantia Book gives five reasons: Paul's organization, thorough Hellenization, the new and great ideal left by Jesus' life bestowal, and two rounds of compromise, first with the Mithraic cult and then with paganism. It matters that the ideal of Jesus stands at the center of this list. Organization and compromise were means; the power that actually drew people came from the life of Jesus. The stage, too, was set. The Western world had inherited a great legacy in philosophy, art, and politics, but “they had no soul-satisfying religion” (195:0.2). Entering that hungry land, Christianity did not remain a simple spiritual appeal; it presented itself as an order for the whole of life."
ch31:17 "Yet the final reason Christianity prevailed was neither theory nor organization but the lives of its believers. The Urantia Book states plainly what gave Christianity its greatest strength."

ch31:20 "Compromise exacted a price. Yet The Urantia Book does not regard it as simple corruption. The leaders laid aside part of Jesus' ideals in order to save many of his ideas, and the result brought both loss and gain. And the revelators insist that what seemed lost has not in fact disappeared."

ch31:23 "The four gains of Christianity were by no means small. The standard of morals was raised, the concept of God was enlarged, and the hope of immortality took its place within religious assurance. Above all, Jesus of Nazareth was given to hungry souls. Many of Jesus' truths were nearly lost in these compromises, but “they yet slumber in this religion of paganized Christianity” (195:0.18)."
ch31:24 "Rome adopted Christ as its moral philosophy. The empire needed a God, and Christianity needed the empire. That union gave Christianity a broad stage, but it also brought the danger of turning it into a culture rather than an experience of the soul."
ch31:26 "The same paragraph goes on to say that many individuals penetrated beneath the surface of this state religion and found within it hidden truths as nourishment for their souls. Even when institution took the place of experience, the individual soul seeking truth still drew living water from within that institution."
ch31:27 "3. The Long Course: Hibernation and Rejuvenation"
ch31:28 "Because the church was bound to society and politics, Christianity declined along with the West in its time of decline. Religion became monasticized and legalized, and the individual all but vanished before the authority of the church. Yet Christianity endured that long night and survived. The Urantia Book compresses all these turns into a single passage."
ch31:30 "The conclusion the revelators draw from this course is not pessimism. The same paragraph says that such a pedigree “is indicative of inherent vitality and the possession of vast recuperative resources” (195:4.4). The source of that vitality is the teaching of Jesus that Christianity carries within it. Institutions, rituals, and philosophies changed their garments from age to age, but the life and words of Jesus wrapped within those garments called forth truth-seeking individuals anew in every generation. It is because of this content that Christianity, time and again on the verge of collapse, came back to life."

ch31:33 "4. Christianity Today: Weaknesses and Strengths"
ch31:34 "The Urantia Book does not conceal the problems of twentieth-century Christianity. Its greatest danger is not persecution from without but a hardening that proceeds from within."
ch31:36 "Another problem is its secondhand character. The revelators say that even Christianity is “not only a religion about Jesus, but it is so largely one which men experience secondhand” (195:9.8). People accept religion just as their teachers hand it down, without going on to experience the presence of God for themselves. Yet this criticism does not end in contempt. The revelators also name honestly the reason modern people avoid the religion of Jesus: it does not leave a person as he is."
ch31:38 "Formalism, organization, and secondhand religion are all ways of softening this demand. When a religion is rationalized, traditionalized, and institutionalized, its power to change me weakens as well. Even so, the text's critique never turns into disdain. The Urantia Book clearly warns against taking Christianity lightly."

ch31:41 "5. The Future: The Way Back to Jesus"
ch31:42 "Beneath this diagnosis lies a single distinction. For the revelators, Christianity's weaknesses come mostly from its distance from the religion of Jesus, and its strengths come mostly from the religion of Jesus that it still holds. The way to remedy the weaknesses and the way to build up the strengths therefore point in the same direction."
ch31:43 "Where, then, does the future of Christianity lie? The Urantia Book's answer is simple: not in returning to primitive Christianity but in moving toward Jesus from where we now stand. Paper 195 says: “It is futile to talk about a revival of primitive Christianity; you must go forward from where you find yourselves.” (195:10.1)"
ch31:45 "Who will lead this renewal? The Urantia Book says it awaits new leaders. They are not managers of institutions but spiritual men and women who rely on Jesus and his teachings alone."
ch31:47 "The picture of the future here is not a reorganization of institutions but a transformation of persons. When spirit-born men and women stand first, the leadership required for the reorganization of society, morals, economics, and politics follows from them (195:9.4). Jesus living again in the experience of believers: that is the substance of Christianity's renewal. And that renewal begins before the very cross Christianity has so long extolled."

ch31:50 "In Closing: The Religion of Jesus Asleep Within the Religion About Jesus"
ch31:51 "The logic of Christianity as The Urantia Book portrays it runs like this. Christianity was born as a religion about the life and teachings of Jesus. Through Hellenization and compromise it won the Roman world, and at that price it obscured part of Jesus' ideals. Yet those ideals did not vanish; they remain latent in his gospel, and because of those latent truths Christianity was able to rise again after its long hibernation. The future of Christianity therefore does not come from outside; it comes from the Jesus it already holds within. The next chapter examines how The Urantia Book itself, which seeks to make this religion of Jesus visible once more, stands in relation to Christianity: what it carries forward and what it enlarges."
