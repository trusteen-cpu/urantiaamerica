ch22.t = Marriage and the Home
ch22.sub = Evolution's Supreme Glory, the First School of Love
ch22.sum = Marriage arose from mankind's adjustment to the two sexes, and its fruit, the home, is the supreme glory of the whole long and arduous evolutionary struggle. The Urantia Book does not sanctify marriage as a sacrament; it regards it as an essentially human institution, yet it says that the ideal mortal marriage is humanly sacred. Man and woman never fully understand each other, yet they need each other forever. The home is the founder of true morality and the place where the child first learns of God the Father. This chapter considers the nature of marriage, the complementarity of the sexes, the home as a school of morality, and the threats and hopes facing the home today.
ch22.s1 = The Origin and Nature of Marriage
ch22.s1.u1.k = The home is the supreme glory of the whole evolutionary struggle and the chief civilizing agency.
ch22.s1.u1.p = Marriage cannot be explained by biological instinct alone. It is a social achievement, the fruit of mankind's long adjustment to the fact of two sexes, and from it the home arose. School, church, and state are all needed, but the child learns the essentials of living above all from home and neighborhood. Civilization depends less on the size of its institutions than on how soundly its homes function.
ch22.s1.u2.k = Marriage is man's most exalted institution, but it is essentially human.
ch22.s1.u2.p = Many religious traditions have regarded marriage as a sacrament joined by God. The Urantia Book corrects this view: the union of husband and wife is a material function of mortals on the evolutionary worlds and is not to be compared with the relation between God and man. Yet this does not make marriage a light matter. Sincere effort within marriage, like every other earnest endeavor, can yield much spiritual progress.
ch22.s1.u3.k = The ideal mortal marriage is humanly sacred, a glorious ideal that lures mankind toward greater happiness.
ch22.s1.u3.p = Seeing marriage as a human institution does not diminish its ideal. On the system capitals the Material Sons and Daughters actually embody that ideal, and earthly marriage grows toward it. Though that dream is seldom fully realized, marriage remains man's supreme dream of temporal ideality, ever luring mankind on to greater strivings for happiness.
ch22.s2 = Man and Woman
ch22.s2.u1.k = Man and woman never fully understand each other, yet they need and complement each other forever.
ch22.s2.u1.p = The difference between the sexes is a gift to mankind before it is a cause of quarrels. Remarkably, the text says this difference does not end with mortal life but continues through the morontia and spirit careers, into Havona and even the Corps of the Finality. Two differing outlooks intrigue, stimulate, and help each other. Marriage is not an institution for erasing this difference but for adjusting it into co-operation.
ch22.s2.u2.k = The real test of marriage is the continuous intimacy that is inescapable in family life.
ch22.s2.u2.p = Courtship may begin in imagination and romance, but marriage is tested in the daily intimacy of living face to face. Two people raised to expect only self-gratification will find it hard to build a lifelong partnership of self-effacement, compromise, and devotion to children. The text points to the contradiction of a modern society that exalts love yet does little to prepare for marriage. Marriage is a matter of character more than of feeling.
ch22.s2.u3.k = Marriage is not an individualistic ideal but the evolving social partnership of a man and a woman.
ch22.s2.u3.p = As women's rights have grown, marriage is developing into an equal partnership founded on sexual fidelity, and this is a great advance. But the text warns against another extreme: a marriage that seeks to monopolize each other's whole personality and individuality is not healthy. Marriage is not only a private ideal of two people; it is also a public partnership functioning within the mores and laws of society.
ch22.s3 = The Home as a School of Morality
ch22.s3.u1.k = Family life is the progenitor of true morality and the first revelation of the heavenly Parent's love.
ch22.s3.u1.p = In the home people learn patience, altruism, tolerance, and forbearance. The enforced association of living with differing personalities stabilizes character and stimulates its growth. But the deepest meaning of the home lies beyond this. Good parents come to understand the Creator's attitude toward his children, and to their children they become the first revelation of the love of the Paradise Parent. The brotherhood of man begins at home.
ch22.s3.u2.k = A child's first concept of the heavenly Father depends wholly on the earthly father.
ch22.s3.u2.p = Jesus' conversation with John Mark makes plain the spiritual weight of the home. The child gains his first impressions of the universe from his mother's care and forms his first picture of God through his father. The love and consistency of parents therefore shape a child's faith before any doctrinal instruction can. The first few years leave a deep mark on the whole of a life.
ch22.s3.u3.k = Any attempt to shift parental responsibility to state or church will prove suicidal to civilization.
ch22.s3.u3.p = In an industrial and urban age the cost of raising children rises, and children easily come to look like an economic burden. Yet the security of civilization depends on one generation's willingness to invest in the next, and that responsibility cannot be handed to other institutions. In place of patriarchal rule the text holds up the Andite family council, where every matter was discussed freely and frankly. A good home rests on brotherly co-operation.
ch22.s4 = Threats and Hopes for the Home
ch22.s4.u1.k = The greatest threat to family life is the rising tide of self-gratification, the modern pleasure mania.
ch22.s4.u1.p = Marriage is the one institution that embraces all three motives of self-maintenance, self-perpetuation, and self-gratification. When the third swallows the other two, the home is shaken. The cravings of the soul cannot be satisfied by physical pleasure, and vanity and fashion have no power to build a home or rear children. This diagnosis speaks deeply to an age that sees marriage merely as a means to personal enjoyment.
ch22.s4.u2.k = Man deserves to enjoy himself, but not at the cost of the home.
ch22.s4.u2.p = The Urantia Book does not teach an asceticism that forbids pleasure; play, rest, and enjoyment are the rightful fruits of the long evolutionary struggle. But the goal of destiny must always be kept in view. If self-gratification destroys marriage and dissolves the home, the price paid is far too high, for the home is man's highest evolutionary achievement and the only hope for the survival of civilization.
ch22.s4.u3.k = The family stood at the center of Jesus' philosophy of life, and true religion and a wise home ennoble each other.
ch22.s4.u3.p = Jesus built his teaching about God in the language of the family: God is Father, men are his children and brothers to one another. He exalted family life as the highest human duty, yet he made it plain that family relationships must not interfere with religious obligations. And the love of a wise home and the devotion of true religion strengthen each other. The home is the first soil in which the gospel takes root.

ch23.t = The State
ch23.sub = Government for the People and the Sovereignty of Mankind
ch23.sum = The state is not an institution handed down from heaven but an evolutionary achievement that society has won from the ravages of war. The Urantia Book values what a government does above the form it takes, and it holds that no state can transcend the moral values of its citizens. The ideal state is moved by the loyalty of love, intelligent patriotism, and cosmic insight; it governs least and co-ordinates most. And if the world is to know lasting peace, it must pass beyond the illusion of unlimited national sovereignty to the sovereignty of mankind. This chapter considers the nature of the state, the ideal state, sovereignty and peace, and the twofold responsibility of being a son of the kingdom and a citizen of an earthly nation.
ch23.s1 = The Origin and Nature of the State
ch23.s1.u1.k = The state is not of divine origin; it is a purely evolutionary institution.
ch23.s1.u1.p = Throughout history many states have presented themselves as sacred orders and demanded absolute loyalty from their citizens. The text strips away that myth. The state is an institution that survived the long struggle for group power, the net gain society has drawn from the sufferings of war. It is useful and necessary, but it is not divine. This recognition is the starting point of a balanced citizenship that neither worships nor despises the state.
ch23.s1.u2.k = The only divine feature of human government is its division into executive, legislative, and judicial domains.
ch23.s1.u2.p = The state itself is not divine, but one principle in it mirrors the way the universe is governed: the division of power into three branches that balance one another. After the long struggle over the concentration of power, it has become clear that evolutionary peoples are best governed by a balanced representative government. Beyond this, the form matters little so long as citizens grow toward self-control and service.
ch23.s1.u3.k = What a state does matters more than what it is, and no state can transcend the morals of its citizens.
ch23.s1.u3.p = Political debate often centers on the names of systems. The text shifts the focus: what matters is not what a state is but what it does. And in the end a government mirrors the leaders its citizens choose; ignorance and selfishness will bring down even the best institutions. A good state therefore begins with good citizens, and reform of the state cannot be separated from the moral growth of individuals.
ch23.s2 = The Ideal State
ch23.s2.u1.k = The ideal state is moved by three impulses: the loyalty of love, intelligent patriotism, and cosmic insight.
ch23.s2.u1.p = The idea of a chosen people once bound tribes together and built nations, but intolerance is forever hostile to human progress. The ideal state rests not on exclusive patriotism but on a loyalty of love born of realizing the brotherhood of man. Patriotism is sound only when founded on wise ideals, and it must be guided by an insight that interprets the planet's needs from a cosmic viewpoint. Love of country finds its rightful place within the love of mankind.
ch23.s2.u2.k = The best state governs the least and co-ordinates the most.
ch23.s2.u2.p = A moral society aims to preserve the self-respect of its citizens and to give everyone an adequate opportunity for self-realization. This is achieved not by heavy control but by minimal regulation and broad co-ordination. Nor is the ideal state imposed from above; it comes as civic consciousness slowly grows. When people who once bore public office as a burden come to seek it as an honored privilege, civilization rises a level.
ch23.s2.u3.k = In the advanced state, political service is esteemed as the highest devotion of citizens.
ch23.s2.u3.p = Cynical contempt for politics ends by handing power to the incompetent and the selfish. The text paints a different picture: the wisest and noblest citizens make public office their highest ambition, and society gives its greatest honors to its public servants. People are free to form their own governments, and with that freedom comes the responsibility to choose those intellectually and morally fit to serve.
ch23.s3 = Sovereignty and World Peace
ch23.s3.u1.k = Citizens are not born for the government; government exists for the benefit of the people.
ch23.s3.u1.p = The revelators restate in modern terms the principles of sovereignty Jesus taught at Urmia. True sovereignty exists on only two levels: the spiritual free will of the individual and the collective sovereignty of mankind as a whole. Every group in between, whether tribe, people, or nation, has value only insofar as it furthers the welfare of the individual and of mankind. When the state makes itself the end, the order is inverted.
ch23.s3.u2.k = War is only a symptom; the real disease is the virus of unlimited national sovereignty.
ch23.s3.u2.p = Many efforts for peace concentrate on reducing arms and signing treaties. The text says these do not touch the root. Even if every weapon were taken away, nations that cling to the absoluteness of their sovereignty would fight with fists and stones. The problem lies not in the instruments but in the idea. To end war, mankind's very concept of sovereignty must change.
ch23.s3.u3.k = A world government of mankind does not surrender sovereignty but creates true sovereignty.
ch23.s3.u3.p = The phrase world government often stirs fear of a vast power that would swallow individual liberty. The text says the opposite. Present nations have never possessed a true sovereignty able to protect them from world war. When local matters are handled locally, national matters nationally, and international matters by a world government, the individual will enjoy far more freedom than now.
ch23.s3.u4.k = Liberty is a gift of civilization made possible by the enforcement of law.
ch23.s3.u4.p = Absolute liberty for one means absolute bondage for another. Liberty is therefore always relative, and it can be shared only under laws that secure equal freedom for all. Religion makes the brotherhood of mankind spiritually possible, but for that brotherhood to be realized in social, economic, and political life, the vessel of government is needed. Faith and institutions do not replace each other; they complete each other.
ch23.s4 = The Kingdom of God and the Earthly State
ch23.s4.u1.k = The Most Highs rule in the kingdoms of men, and there is no chosen people.
ch23.s4.u1.p = Earthly politics may look like a purely human affair, but the text says the rule of the Most Highs stands above it. That rule favors no particular people; it is designed to bring the greatest good to the greatest number for the longest time. No nation, then, can present itself as God's sole instrument. The spiritual sovereignty of God takes precedence over every intermediate loyalty.
ch23.s4.u2.k = Because he is a son of the heavenly kingdom, the believer should be a better citizen of the earthly state.
ch23.s4.u2.p = Jesus taught that there is no conflict between sonship in the kingdom of God and citizenship on earth. The believer bears duties to man and to God, and willingly takes on a third, the service of the brotherhood. Faith does not draw us out of the world; it makes us more honest and devoted citizens. Yet we must refuse to worship rulers or to use the power of the state for spiritual ends.
